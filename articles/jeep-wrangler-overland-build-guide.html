Why the Wrangler Is the Overland Icon

No vehicle defines overlanding culture like the Jeep Wrangler. It's been going places it has no business going since 1941, and it hasn't stopped. The Wrangler's combination of solid front and rear axles, short wheelbase, high ground clearance, and a modular design that's been purpose-built for modification for over 80 years makes it unlike anything else on the market.

The Wrangler benefits from an extensive aftermarket ecosystem with many bolt-on solutions for common modifications. Established suppliers offer kits for axle swaps, large-diameter tires, onboard air systems, and locker control. That variety makes it straightforward to source parts for specific goals, but it also increases the potential to spend heavily on upgrades that may not produce proportional capability gains.

This guide focuses on modifications that are likely to produce meaningful capability improvements, organized into three budget levels to help prioritize upgrades.

What Makes the Wrangler Different

Before diving into the builds, it's worth understanding what makes the Wrangler uniquely suited — and occasionally uniquely challenging — as an overland platform.

Solid axles front and rear: Unlike virtually every other modern vehicle in the segment, the Wrangler runs solid beam axles on both ends. This means the suspension geometry stays consistent through full articulation, lift doesn't introduce CV angle problems, and axle repairs on the trail are straightforward. It also means the on-road handling is distinctly trucky compared to an independent-front-suspension platform like the Tacoma or 4Runner.

Factory locker availability: The Rubicon trim comes with Dana 44 axles and electronic lockers front and rear from the factory. This is extraordinary capability that typically costs $2,000–$4,000 to add to a vehicle that doesn't have it. If serious off-road performance is your goal, a Rubicon as a starting point is often the most cost-effective path.

Fold-down windshield and removable doors/top: Provides open-air driving and unobstructed views useful for photography and navigation; this configuration is valued by some overlanders for the direct connection to surroundings.

Dana axle options: JK Wranglers (2007–2018) come with Dana 30/44 (Sport, Sahara) or Dana 44/44 (Rubicon) axles. JL Wranglers (2018–present) get Dana 30/35 (Sport), Dana 44/44 (Sahara, Sport S), or Dana 44 front / Dana 44 AdvanTEK rear (Rubicon). For serious wheeling, the Rubicon's Dana 44s are far preferable to the lighter 30/35 axles.

For budget‑focused trail builds, a clean JK Rubicon can offer value because of a large aftermarket, common Dana 44 axles, and generally lower parts costs that allow more budget for modifications. Buyers seeking a daily driver that also performs well on trails may prefer the JL for its improved fuel economy, modern features, and more refined highway behavior.

33s vs 35s vs 37s — The Wrangler Tire Guide

33" tires (255/75R17 or 285/70R17): Fit stock or mildly lifted JK/JL Wranglers without rubbing. Best choice for daily drivers who do occasional trails — minimal fuel economy penalty, no driveline stress, and stock wheels are fine. Most Rubicons can fit 33s from the factory. For casual overlanding, 33s are entirely adequate.

35" tires (315/70R17 or 35x12.50R17): The sweet spot for most serious overlanders. Require 2.5"–3.5" of lift for good clearance. Fit the factory Dana 44 axle shafts without undue stress. Meaningfully better flotation in sand and mud than 33s. Minor gearing consideration — most builds at 35s will want a re-gear to 4.56 or 4.88 to restore highway RPM and towing capacity. The 35" BFG KO2 and KM3 are both excellent in this size.

37" tires (37x12.50R17):): Intended for high‑clearance, off‑road–focused builds. Typically require 4" or more of lift and may need front fender trimming on many JL trims. The increased unsprung weight and rotational inertia can place additional stress on factory axle shafts; owners planning frequent use with 37" tires should budget for upgraded axle shafts or an axle swap. Re‑gearing (commonly to 5.13) is often recommended. Capability increases over 35" tires are most notable on rock and in deep mud, while on‑road compromises increase—making 37" tires better suited to dedicated trail rigs than daily drivers.

Wrangler Overland FAQ

Choosing between JK and JL Wrangler platforms for overlanding depends on priorities such as aftermarket support, vehicle electronics, ride characteristics, and budget. Older platforms often offer widespread aftermarket parts and mechanical simplicity, which can simplify repairs on long trips. Newer platforms typically provide updated comfort, safety features, and factory technologies that can benefit long‑distance travel and on‑road comfort. Evaluate intended use (technical trails versus extended overland travel), payload and cargo needs, availability of replacement parts, and personal preference for modern conveniences when deciding.

Both platforms have strengths. The JK Rubicon (2012–2018 with the 3.6L Pentastar) offers Dana 44 axles and factory lockers and benefits from an established aftermarket that can lower modification costs. The JL provides improved fuel economy, updated technology, and refined highway behavior, and available safety data indicate improved crash performance in several areas. For buyers prioritizing daily usability and long‑term refinement, the JL is typically the stronger all‑around option; for buyers prioritizing lower initial cost for modification, a well‑maintained JK Rubicon can be a strong value.

Highway driving suitability

The Wrangler’s upright windshield and boxy profile increase aerodynamic drag, which reduces fuel efficiency at highway speeds; roof racks or rooftop tents and high‑profile tires further increase drag and road noise. With appropriate tires and maintenance, a Wrangler can sustain highway speeds comfortably, but fuel consumption and noise levels are generally higher than those of a car or crossover. JL models equipped with the 2.0T engine or a diesel option offer improved highway fuel efficiency. For users who spend extensive time on highways, a body‑on‑frame truck platform may be a more efficient choice overall.

Best tires for overlanding on a Wrangler?

All‑terrain recommendation: BFGoodrich All‑Terrain T/A KO2 is widely cited as a benchmark AT, offering balanced dry and wet road performance, capable off‑road behavior, and a 50,000‑mile treadwear warranty. Mixed‑terrain/expedition recommendation: BFGoodrich Mud‑Terrain T/A KM3 provides a more aggressive tread and a more rugged sidewall for improved mud and rock traction at the expense of increased road noise and rolling resistance. Dedicated mud/rock options include Falken Wildpeak MT and Toyo Open Country MT, which are noted for robust compounds and reinforced sidewalls.
