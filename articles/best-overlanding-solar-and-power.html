Why a Dedicated Power System Matters for Overlanding

Typical overlanding electrical loads—using manufacturer-listed current draws as a guide—include a compressor fridge (~3–5A continuous), a rooftop‑tent fan or heater (2–8A), LED camp lighting (0.5–2A), device charging (1–3A), and CPAP machines (5–10A). Combined, those items can total roughly 15–25+ A of sustained draw, which may exceed what a starter battery and short alternator‑only charging periods can reliably support.

A dedicated auxiliary power system provides an isolated house battery to run camp electronics without risking the starter battery. Such systems are typically recharged via driving (alternator or DC‑DC charging) and/or solar input.

Three main approaches have distinct strengths: portable power stations, dual-battery setups, and full house lithium battery systems.

  • Portable power station: No installation, works at camp and at home, easy to upgrade. Best for overlanders who don't want to modify their vehicle or who haven't committed to a single truck.
  • Dual battery system (under-hood AGM or lithium): Permanently installed, charges while driving, and offers higher continuous output suitable for sustained high-draw loads; commonly chosen for permanently modified rigs.
  • Full lithium house battery: Maximum capacity and efficiency, best for long expeditions. Higher upfront cost and installation complexity.

1. Goal Zero Yeti 1500 Portable Power Station (6th Gen) — Best All-in-One Power Station

Goal Zero Yeti 1500 (6th Gen): manufacturer-listed 1,516 Wh capacity. That capacity can provide substantial runtime for a compressor fridge; actual runtime depends on fridge settings, ambient temperature, and usage of other loads.

A pure sine wave inverter reduces voltage distortion compared with modified sine designs, making it appropriate for sensitive electronics (CPAPs, laptops, camera chargers). App connectivity allows remote monitoring of input/output power and scheduling or configuration of compatible devices.

The home-use case is underrated. When you're not overlanding, the Yeti 1500 (6th Gen) sits in your garage fully charged as a home backup power station. During a grid outage, it powers your refrigerator, phone charging, and lighting for 12–24 hours. That dual utility helps justify the $1,999 price tag.

A DC-DC charger (battery-to-battery) is the appropriate method to charge an auxiliary lithium battery from a vehicle alternator. Modern smart alternators vary output voltage in ways that can prevent simple isolator relays from delivering an optimal charge; DC-DC chargers accommodate variable alternator behavior and provide multi-stage charge profiles suited to lithium chemistries. A unit rated for 50A can supply up to 50A of charging current; actual recharge time depends on the auxiliary battery’s state of charge, chemistry, and the charger’s programmed profile.

Portable Power Station vs. Permanent Dual Battery System

Which option is better depends on individual needs, vehicle use, and trip plans.

Choose a portable power station if:

  • You don't want to modify your vehicle
  • Operate multiple vehicles or have not committed to a single overland build.
  • You're just getting started and want to try the overlanding lifestyle before investing in permanent mods
  • You want the unit to pull double duty as home backup power
  • You're doing trips of 4 nights or fewer where recharge from solar covers your needs

Choose a permanent dual battery system if:

  • Have a dedicated overland vehicle intended for long‑term outfitting.
  • You're doing longer trips (5+ nights) where you want maximum capacity
  • You want the power system to be invisible and always ready — no setup, no lifting heavy stations
  • You're running high-draw loads (air compressor, inverter for cooking) that exceed portable station output limits
  • You're mounting a roof-top solar panel and want a clean, permanent wiring solution

How to Calculate Your Power Needs

Add up the watt-hours consumed by each device over a 24-hour camp period:

  • Compressor fridge (Dometic CFX3 35): ~2A x 12V x 24h x 40% duty cycle = ~230 Wh/day
  • Rooftop tent fan: ~1.5A x 12V x 8h = ~144 Wh/night
  • LED camp lighting: ~10W x 4h = ~40 Wh/night
  • Phone charging (x2): ~20W x 2h = ~40 Wh/day
  • CPAP (if applicable): ~30–50W x 8h = ~240–400 Wh/night

A typical overlander without a CPAP runs 450–550 Wh per day. With a CPAP, plan for 700–950 Wh per day. Size your battery bank to cover 1.5–2 days of consumption without recharge — that gives you a buffer for cloudy days and ensures you never drain the battery dangerously low.

Solar Charging Math

For planning purposes, peak sun hours vary by location and season. Use a local solar resource estimate when sizing a system:

  • 100W panel: ~400 Wh/day in good conditions
  • 200W panel: ~800 Wh/day in good conditions
  • 400W panel (two 200W suitcases): ~1,600 Wh/day in good conditions

A single 200W panel generates approximately 800 Wh on a good sunny day — enough to fully replace the consumption of a running compressor fridge plus basic devices. Add shade, clouds, or sub-optimal panel angle and that drops significantly. For serious off-grid use in variable conditions, 300–400W of solar capacity is the practical minimum for an overlander running a fridge and base camp electronics.

Lithium vs. AGM vs. Lead-Acid

Short version: buy lithium (LiFePO4) if you can afford it. Here's the detailed comparison:

  • Flooded lead-acid: Cheapest upfront ($80–$150 for 100Ah). Heavy, maintenance-required, requires ventilation, 30–50% usable capacity, 300–500 cycles. Only makes sense as a cheap starter solution you plan to replace.
  • AGM (Absorbed Glass Mat): No maintenance, sealed, tolerates some vibration. 50% usable capacity, 500–800 cycles. $150–$250 for 100Ah. A reasonable budget choice that lasts 2–3 years of regular use.
  • LiFePO4 (Lithium Iron Phosphate): Manufacturer-listed ratings often cite ~80–90% usable capacity and roughly 3,000–5,000 cycles; many models include an integrated BMS and are significantly lighter than lead‑acid. Typical retail prices for quality 100Ah LiFePO4 units are around $800–$1,000. Higher upfront cost but often lower cost per cycle, making LiFePO4 well suited for permanent installations.

Power System FAQ

How long will a power station run my overlanding fridge?

It depends on ambient temperature, how often the lid is opened, and how full the fridge is. As a rule of thumb: a Dometic CFX3 35 at 77°F ambient draws about 230 Wh per day. A Goal Zero Yeti 1500 (6th Gen) (1,516 Wh usable) would run that fridge for approximately 6.5 days with nothing else drawing power, or about 2 days in a typical camp scenario with lights and device charging. A Jackery Explorer 1000 v2 (1,002 Wh) gives you roughly 4 days fridge-only or 1.5 days of full camp use. At hotter temperatures, expect 20–40% shorter runtimes.

Is solar enough to power a setup while driving?

Solar production while driving is variable and depends on panel placement, sun angle, and shading; roof-mounted or deployed panels may provide only modest output while in motion. That output can offset refrigerator load when parked in good sun but generally will not fully recharge a deeply depleted battery bank by itself. A combined approach—using solar for stationary top-ups and an alternator-supplied DC-DC charger for bulk recharging—is commonly recommended. Depending on system capacity, daily driving, and available sunlight, this combination can support an auxiliary battery system.

What's the difference between a power station and a dual battery system?

A portable power station is a self‑contained unit combining battery, inverter, charge controller, and monitoring in a portable enclosure and requires no vehicle installation; it is charged at home or via solar and is carried in and out of the vehicle. A dual battery system is a permanent installation: a second battery wired into the vehicle electrical system, isolated from the starter battery, usually charged by the alternator through a DC-DC charger and optionally by rooftop solar. Portable stations provide flexibility and no vehicle modification; dual battery systems provide always‑available capacity and are typically preferred for high‑capacity, long‑expedition setups.
